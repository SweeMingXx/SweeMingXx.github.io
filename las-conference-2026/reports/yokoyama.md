Genealogies of Speech Technology, Labor Invisibilization, and Resisting Algorithmic Homogenization

## Executive Summary & Theoretical Architecture
Closing Day 1 of the LAS Conference 2026, Assistant Professor Setsuko Yokoyama delivered a profound, humanistic interrogation of the artificial intelligence economy. Drawing upon her dual identity as a literary historian and algorithmic fairness researcher at Singapore University of Technology and Design (SUTD), Yokoyama reframed contemporary AI anxieties not as unprecedented ruptures, but as the latest chapter in a thirty-year intertwined genealogy spanning open-source software, digital humanities, and library science.

Yokoyama argued that current generative AI and automated speech recognition (ASR) systems are heavily encoded with corporate business models—specifically the historical, gendered mechanization of secretarial labor. When institutions outsource data development to commercial tech monopolies, they commodify language and invisibilize essential human annotation labor. Countering this extractive paradigm, Yokoyama presented "Communities, Not Commodities"—advocating for participatory DIY data stewardship, indigenous algorithmic sovereignty, and repositioning libraries as critical infrastructures that reveal the mechanical parameters hidden behind the facade of "AI magic."

### 1. Deconstructing AI Metaphors
AI is not an autonomous sentient force, but statistical pattern matching and probability models built on historical human cultural codes.

### 2. Invisibilized Labor & Care
Highlighting ghost workers, stenographers, and disability access transcribers who transform speech-to-text into care work and artistic expression.

### 3. Digital Sovereignty
Championing community-governed AI ecosystems (e.g., Te Hiku Media) where language revitalization takes precedence over commercial extraction.

## A Thirty-Year Intertwined Genealogy (1990s – 2020s)
Yokoyama traced three decades of technological evolution shared by libraries and the digital humanities. The 1990s Open Access (OA) movement emerged from the software open-source ethos (e.g., Richard Stallman's Copyleft), seeking to democratize research access and overturn print gatekeeping. Early digital literary projects—such as Marta Werner and Martha Nell Smith recovering Emily Dickinson's manuscripts—revealed how print publishing had smoothed away the poet's visual eccentricities and expressive erasures, demonstrating that mediation always constructs authority.

By the 2010s, libraries matured OA by conceptualizing "Collections as Data," enabling computational linguistic analysis at scale and creative remixes (such as Brian Foo's Citizen DJ at the Library of Congress). However, scholars like Kimberley Christen challenged uncritical open access, proving that categorizing indigenous cultural knowledge as "public domain" functioned as an extension of colonial exploitation, prompting the creation of the Mukurtu CMS and Traditional Knowledge (TK) labels.

### Genealogical evolution of library & digital humanities commons
- 1990s: Open Access — Democratizing Primary Texts
- 2010s: Collections as Data — Computational Scale & TK Labels
- 2020s: Web Archiving — Crisis Scraping (SUCHO, Data Rescue)
- 2026: Sovereign AI — Communities, Not Commodities

## The 2020s Scraping Crisis & Reverse-Engineering SEA-LION
In the 2020s, digital humanities encountered the era of web archiving and mass scraping. Initiatives like SUCHO (Saving Ukrainian Cultural Heritage Online) and North America's Data Rescue mobilized the archival principle "Lots of Copies Keep Stuff Safe" (LOCKSS) to protect vulnerable heritage from military invasion and state censorship.

Simultaneously, commercial software engineers began scraping the open web as raw training fodder for Large Language Models. Conducting a reverse-engineering analysis of AI Singapore's regional model SEA-LION, Yokoyama examined how developers extracted Southeast Asian tokens from Google's mC4 and Common Crawl. She warned that uncurated web scraping inherits internet prejudices, highlighting the urgent imperative to audit the underlying datasets fueling generative engines.

## Labor History: The Gendered Stenographer & Ghost Workers
Yokoyama challenged the pervasive assumption that automation naturally displaces human workers. Deconstructing automated transcription, she demonstrated that current AI tools (e.g., Zoom automated notes, Otter.ai) uncritically replicate mid-century secretarial corporate norms—evidenced in 1947 training films where female stenographers executed structured, subordinate dictation.

Citing historian Gregory Downey, Yokoyama noted that 19th-century court stenography was a prestigious, highly paid male occupation with professional associations. It was only when women entered the field—coinciding with the introduction of mechanical typewriters—that capital downgraded transcription to "menial" labor. Today, this devaluation persists through Mary L. Gray and Siddharth Suri's concept of ghost work: invisibilized global gig workers (via Amazon Mechanical Turk) who clean, annotate, and moderate datasets behind automated facades.

|---|---|---|
| Corporate ASR / AI (Siri, Zoom, Big Tech) | Mechanized corporate secretarial dictation; gig-economy ghost workers; cost cutting. | Standardized Anglo-centric efficiency; erases dialects, accents, and vernacular variance. |

## Indigenous Sovereignty: The Te Hiku Media Benchmark
Countering commodification, Yokoyama showcased New Zealand's Te Hiku Media, an indigenous Māori broadcasting collective. Recognizing that commercial speech recognition failed to capture indigenous phonology, they curated 30 years of oral archives to train an in-house Māori ASR engine achieving an extraordinary 92% accuracy rate, outperforming global tech conglomerates.

### Indigenous Data Sovereignty: "Nobody Knows Us Better Than Ourselves"
Te Hiku's breakthrough stems from deep linguistic intimacy: Māori vocabulary contains over 200 distinct names for winds and rains that generic corporate algorithms cannot comprehend. Yokoyama urged Singapore to adopt an identical participatory model for preserving endangered heritage dialects—Teochew, Hokkien, Cantonese, and Baba Malay—through intergenerational co-design rather than corporate outsourcing.

By keeping the data curation process within the community, AI becomes an empowering instrument of cultural transmission and self-determination rather than an extractive corporate commodity.

## Key Takeaways & Core Strategic Insights
### Executive takeaways
- Communities over Commodities: Reclaim participatory AI development rather than outsourcing linguistic models to Big Tech monopolies.
- Labor Invisibilization: AI rests on historical gendered deskilling and underpaid gig workers (ghost work) performing critical data annotation.
- Indigenous Sovereignty (Te Hiku): Localized, community-governed datasets achieve 92% ASR accuracy, outperforming corporate models.
- Revealing AI Parameters: Apply classical library source criticism to expose the indexing bounds, scraping origins, and bias of AI tools.

### Core strategic insights
- The Metaphor Trap: Deconstruct "AI" into specific statistical mechanics (probability engines) to dismantle false promises of automated infallibility.
- Language as Living Heritage: Speech-to-text is care work and cultural pride, requiring human-in-the-loop co-design for local dialects.
- Resisting Homogenization: Counter corporate citation loops and generic outputs through curated humanities collections and personal libraries.
- The Pedagogical "Aha" Moment: Teach digital-native patrons to examine AI as a mediated object rather than an objective authority.
